# HeyHood: Indian investor brief and pitch preparation

**Working brief · 8 October 2026 · Read with [the full product strategy](HEYHOOD_PRODUCT_STRATEGY_INDIA.md)**

This is a candid preparation document, not a claim of fundraising readiness, investor interest, paying traction or existing partnerships. The founder's vision is a chat-first consumer platform that understands a community, connects its supply and demand, and brings in missing services. Discovery/subscriptions come first; booked services and integrated household commerce are later horizons.

## 1. The company in one sentence

**HeyHood helps residents find what their community can offer and brings in what it repeatedly needs, through one simple chat.**

A more commercial version:

**A community discovery and services platform that turns local intent into useful matches and qualified demand for providers.**

The first version is easier to understand; the second helps introduce the business model. Do not open with an inventory of features, “an AI-powered super app” or the entire household-services TAM.

## 2. Elevator pitches

### Approximately 30 seconds

> Large Indian apartment communities already have home sellers, tutors and service providers, but residents discover them through scattered groups and word of mouth. HeyHood gives residents one simple chat to find relevant supply in their hood. When a community repeatedly searches for a service it lacks, we can qualify that demand and bring in a suitable provider. We’re testing a business around provider subscriptions and useful service activations. The long-term advantage we want to build is maintained community context and a repeatable ability to turn missing demand into reliable supply.

This is forward-looking wording. If the activation and commercial experiment have not yet happened, retain “can,” “testing” and “want to build.” Do not replace them with proven-growth language.

### Approximately 90 seconds

> A gated community with thousands of flats already has a local economy: someone sells batter, someone teaches Hindi, someone does makeup, and neighbours share airport rides. A new resident often finds none of that because the information is spread across WhatsApp groups, personal contacts and old menus.
>
> HeyHood starts with a familiar chat experience. A resident says what they need, in their language, and gets relevant community choices with current offering information and a way to connect. Rides and plans use the same matching idea, with their own time and participation constraints.
>
> The larger opportunity is the context this creates. If several different households need a car wash and there is no suitable supply, that becomes a sourcing signal. We can qualify a common service window, bring in a reliable team, measure the outcome, and repeat. Providers could pay for relevant reach and service activations across a cluster of communities.
>
> We have a live pilot product with discovery, storefronts, rides/plans and connection feedback/reviews. Much of the current supply is demo data, so the next proof is real demand, real connections, a paid supplier experiment and repeatability in a second community. Later we can add confirmed service bookings and partner integrations for at-home diagnostics, wellness and medicine delivery, while keeping the consumer front door simple.

In a live pitch, replace the last paragraph with dated, verified metrics once they exist. Never turn a deployed application into a claim of demonstrated commerce.

### If an investor says “So, Justdial for communities?”

> That is a useful starting comparison for discovery and provider subscriptions. Our hypothesis is that community-specific offerings, availability and repeated unmet intent let us make more useful matches and activate concentrated service demand. We need to prove that through connections, provider economics and repeatable launches—not just a directory.

### If they say “So, UrbanClap?”

> Urban Company is the relevant services comparison. We are beginning earlier in the workflow, with community discovery and qualified demand. If we later handle bookings, we must build the confirmation, capacity and service operations to support that promise. We are not claiming that managed-service capability today.

### If they say “So, a D2C healthcare app?”

> Healthcare is a later partner vertical, not the opening business. The consumer accesses partner services through HeyHood; the business could be distribution or marketplace economics rather than an own-brand D2C model. We would start with one authorised workflow and actual partner confirmation.

## 3. Where this sits in the Indian startup ecosystem

HeyHood can be interpreted through several lenses. The fundraising story should follow the business being proven, rather than whichever label attracts the most attention.

| Lens | What the investor expects | Evidence HeyHood would need |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer utility/community product | Frequent useful behaviour, retention and low-friction distribution | Resident cohorts returning for actual needs; community-level density |
| Local marketplace/lead business | Reliable matching, paying providers and sound unit economics | Incremental qualified connections/jobs, renewals and contribution |
| Community SaaS/B2B2C | A budget owner, sales cycle, durable contract and clear community value | Actual RWA/builder purchase and renewal—not only permission to pilot |
| Services marketplace | Real acceptance, capacity, completion, cancellation and quality operations | Partner agreements, reliable workflows and outcome attribution |
| Applied AI | AI improves the job, with manageable cost and defensible context/workflow | Better intent/language matching than alternatives; measured quality and cost |

**Recommended current framing:** a community discovery and provider-demand business using conversational AI. It has an option to become a deeper services/partner-commerce platform if the demand and operations justify it.

The 2026 Bain/IVCA India VC report covers 2025. It describes a more measured consumer-tech environment, interest in vertical platforms, retention-led growth, disciplined unit economics and applied/vertical AI. For HeyHood, the useful implication is to prove a focused demand/retention business before presenting a broad platform vision. It does not establish October 2026 fund appetite, a current investment multiple or any particular investor's interest.

## 4. The investment thesis and its proof burden

### What is attractive

- Dense communities may concentrate users, providers and visiting-service opportunities geographically.
- Residents already transact informally; HeyHood need not invent every category of demand.
- Newcomers and language-fragmented groups expose a tangible discovery problem.
- Persistent supply plus fresh availability can make the experience more useful than a static directory.
- Repeated unmet needs can guide supply recruitment and service-window aggregation.
- One person can supply and seek across contexts, supporting reciprocal community use.
- A simple chat can hide the complexity of multiple supplier systems while retaining grounded choices and explicit confirmation.

Each point is a hypothesis until the corresponding behaviour is measured. Geographic density does not automatically produce an engaged digital network.

### Why an investor could pass

- It looks like a feature of MyGate/NoBrokerHood rather than a standalone company.
- Another app adds friction to habits already served by WhatsApp.
- Sellers and service teams have low willingness to pay or see little incremental business.
- Each community needs manual acquisition/curation; economics do not transfer.
- Low per-community revenue requires an impractically large operating footprint.
- The founder's moat is data that incumbents already possess or can obtain.
- Outside service activation becomes a local agency with labour-heavy margins.
- “AI misses” are technical failures incorrectly presented as commercial demand.
- WhatsApp handoff prevents reliable booking/revenue attribution.
- The roadmap expands into rides, events, health and medicines before one paid repeatable use case is established.

The response is not a longer feature list. It is evidence that one community repeatedly produces useful matches and sustainable value, then evidence that another can do the same.

### The founder's strongest differentiated thesis

**Maintained community context + qualified unmet demand + reliable supply activation.**

Explain the three terms separately:

1. Context knows what is relevant in this place now.
2. Qualified demand distinguishes genuine gaps from repeats, timing mismatch and retrieval bugs.
3. Supply activation turns that gap into an accepted service window and observed outcomes.

A query log is useful input; a working economic loop is the stronger defensibility argument. Three or four misses can trigger investigation. They do not prove four purchases, justify guaranteed provider fees or establish a durable moat by themselves.

MyGate's public pages describe Buy & Sell and community advertising. ApnaComplex Bazaar presents service offers and provider listing/recommendation actions. These are important direct overlaps. Do not pitch “community marketplace,” “service partners” or “advertising” as uniquely new inventions.

Vendor home-page scale claims and app download counts are not comparable to HeyHood's active residents or service outcomes. We have not done logged-in competitive usability testing, so absence of a feature from a landing page is not evidence the feature does not exist.

## 6. Revenue narrative: discovery first, deeper commerce later

### Opening commercial hypothesis

Start by testing provider/business subscriptions or a clearly defined paid activation. The resident gets useful discovery; the provider pays when it earns relevant reach and repeat value. Do not assume consumers will pay merely to search their hood.

Subscription packages might eventually relate to specified community coverage, genuine availability, qualified demand reporting or supported activations. Unlimited access to every hood is not an economical promise if the provider cannot serve it or if each listing needs human work. Tier definitions and prices require testing.

Earlier founder ideas—₹99/₹299 monthly seller tiers, ₹2,499 annual, promotions and licensing—are hypotheses. The live product does not establish collected recurring revenue.

### Blue-collar service pathway

The candidate paying customer can be an individual skilled provider, a small team, a contractor or an organised service business. They have different job values, margins, travel costs and capacity. Do not treat the entire sector as one ARPU.

The pitch-worthy evidence is:

- Which qualified gap led to the provider activation?
- How many households explicitly wanted that service/window?
- How many accepted and completed a job?
- What did the provider earn incrementally and what did servicing cost?
- What did HeyHood collect and spend?
- Did the provider renew or request another community/service window?

Illustrative break-even reasoning: if a provider contributes ₹100 per genuinely incremental job before the platform fee, a ₹299 monthly fee needs roughly three such jobs merely to cover that fee. These are assumed figures, not car-wash pricing or pilot margins. Provider retention needs a stronger value proposition than barely breaking even.

### Partner-commerce horizon

Diagnostics sample collection, home wellness and medicine fulfilment can later broaden household utility. Provider/partner subscription, referral and transaction economics are separate hypotheses; they should not all be added to near-term forecast revenue.

The consumer experience can remain a chat while the platform integrates serviceability, slots, pricing, explicit confirmation and authoritative status. Partner terms determine who provides the service, collects payment, handles changes and owns the clinical/pharmacy obligations. A promoted offer is not a medical recommendation or a confirmed booking.

Third-party distribution to consumers is not automatically an own-brand D2C business. Describe it precisely as a consumer-facing partner marketplace/distribution channel when that is what the agreements support.

## 7. Market and scale story

Use a bottom-up model: **eligible reachable communities × realised annual revenue per active community**. Establish the community count, access rate, activation rate and renewal assumptions. Founder-proposed Tridasa/Sayuk flat counts are target-market context, not active-user metrics or a signed distribution base.

The full document models ₹5,965/month per hood without RWA fees and ₹8,965/month with a hypothetical licence. At 1,000 active paying communities, those scenarios produce approximately ₹7.16 crore and ₹10.76 crore annual revenue respectively, before costs. They are scenario arithmetic—not TAM, forecast or proof that 1,000 such communities can be reached.

This makes an investor's scale question concrete. A viable focused business can precede a venture-scale business. Bigger outcomes need greater revenue intensity, wider repeatable distribution or a proven supplier network. Adding the national food, health and services TAMs together does not answer that question.

Do not quote a funding ask or valuation without the team's actual hiring/operating plan. Calculate the ask from runway, net burn, setup costs and a defined evidence milestone. No round size, valuation or dilution is proposed here because those inputs have not been supplied.

## 10. Fundraising readiness and possible route

These are suggested readiness levels, not universal investor thresholds.

- **Founder/angel pilot discussion:** tangible problem, working product, credible community access and a specific test plan. Be explicit about demo supply and unproven revenue.
- **Pre-seed evidence discussion:** genuine cohorts/supply, repeat useful connections, a first paid activation/subscription experiment, costs and a clear founder operating advantage.
- **Institutional seed discussion:** repeated cohorts, paying renewal, a transferable launch/activation playbook and unit economics after non-founder operating costs. Native apps and more modules alone do not establish this level.

Potential investor audiences include relevant operator angels and early consumer/marketplace investors; strategic community/service partners may also help distribution. Their participation is unconfirmed. A narrower profitable business and a VC-backed expansion path are different legitimate outcomes.

Before reaching out, assemble a compact diligence pack:

1. Product/strategy and current-status documents.
2. Real provider/community permission records and consented listing practices.
3. Anonymised cohort, query-quality, connection and gap-activation metrics.
4. Actual provider payments/renewals and the definition of a billed lead/activation.
5. Community acquisition/curation hours and real infrastructure costs.
6. Source-based market sizing and comparison results.
7. Team/IP ownership, capital needs and a milestone-linked financial plan.
8. Partner agreements and responsibility mapping when booking/health/medicine integrations are proposed.

Do not put private resident phone numbers, invitation codes, prescriptions, API secrets or screenshots containing credentials into the pitch or diligence copy.

## 12. Recommended next proof for the founder

Run one complete, honest story:

**Several distinct resident needs → accurate gap classification → explicit compatible interest → a suitable visiting service team → confirmed service window → reported/completed outcomes → provider payment and repeat willingness.**

Choose a practical category such as car washing or bicycle servicing, then repeat the experiment in a second hood with recorded human effort. Keep the same simple chat entry point. That story would connect the problem, the moat thesis, the blue-collar opportunity and the revenue model more convincingly than another dozen modules.
